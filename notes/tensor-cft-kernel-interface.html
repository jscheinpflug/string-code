1. Goal

This note rewrites the proposed interface for tensor-code and cft-code in plain physics language.

The kernel should support the worldsheet calculations needed by Overview:

  • free-field OPEs and correlators without storing huge intermediate sums;
  • RNS spin fields and excited Ramond-sector OPEs;
  • on-shell amplitudes and SFT brackets as clients of the same CFT operations;
  • boundary and orientifold projections;
  • weak-background/NLSM perturbation theory;
  • pure-spinor worldsheet calculations.

The guiding point is simple:

  • cft-code describes local worldsheet operators and computes their OPEs, projections, and correlators.
  • tensor-code describes spacetime/internal tensor structures that appear as coefficients of those worldsheet calculations.

The CFT tensor product is handled exactly as in the current direction: an operator carries a list of quantum-number entries. A product theory just gives operators longer lists of charges, sectors, and representation labels. There is no need for a separate runtime object for the tensor product of CFT sectors.

2. Kernel Boundary

cft-code should know about:

  • local fields and local operators;
  • insertion points \(z,\bar z,x\) and derivatives;
  • normal-ordered products;
  • conformal weights;
  • quantum numbers as a list of named entries;
  • which fields can contract with which other fields;
  • which evaluation strategies each operator kind supports;
  • the preference order of strategies in each sector;
  • projection of an OPE to a target weight;
  • OPEs and correlators of any number of local insertions;
  • the surface on which a correlator is evaluated;
  • finite projections such as GSO/orbifold/orientifold actions;
  • sector-level statistics and cocycle conventions;
  • BCFT data: boundary conditions, their boundary operator kinds, and gluing data for the doubling trick.

cft-code should not know the physical interpretation of the entries called ghost_number, picture, GSO, pure_spinor_ghost_number, or any similar name. They are just quantum numbers in a list.

tensor-code should know about:

  • symmetry algebras such as \(SO(10)\), \(Spin(1,9)\), internal symmetry algebras, Chan-Paton groups, and \(U(1)\)’s;
  • representations such as vector, spinor, conjugate spinor, adjoint, and singlet;
  • abstract indices;
  • invariant tensors such as \(\eta_{\mu\nu}\), \(\delta^\mu{}_\nu\), \(\epsilon_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_d}\), charge conjugation matrices, antisymmetrized gamma matrices, generators, and Clebsch-Gordan coefficients;
  • linear combinations of such tensor structures;
  • generation of all allowed invariant tensors;
  • selection of an independent tensor basis;
  • solving for coefficients in that basis.

tensor-code should not know what an OPE, a PCO, a string bracket, or a worldsheet ghost is.

3. Redline Against Current Org

This section records where this proposal differs from, or extends, the current /org code.

3.1. Differences

  1. Current Symmetry already treats quantum numbers as a list of representation labels. This design keeps that point. The previous draft incorrectly suggested product-algebra metadata as if CFT tensor products needed a separate object. They do not. For CFT purposes, tensor products are represented by concatenating quantum-number lists.
  2. Current Symmetry uses small ids such as u8. This design differs only by recommending wider ids for long calculations. This is a storage choice, not a physics change.
  3. Current Symmetry has only none and one Kronecker delta as tensor structures. This design differs by requiring general tensor structures: products and sums of metrics, deltas, epsilons, gamma matrices, generators, and Clebsch-Gordan tensors.
  4. Current Coefficient uses floating scalars. This design differs by requiring exact symbolic scalars for kernel data. Conformal weights, OPE powers, and spin-field branch powers are rational data, not floating-point data.
  5. Current Coefficient uses integer powers in rational functions. This design differs by allowing rational powers. Ramond spin fields and bosonized exponentials naturally produce fractional powers.
  6. Current Operators represents a local operator as an insertion and a body. This design keeps that. The difference is that the body store must have one canonical constructor for normal-ordered products, so signs, repeated fermions, and same-point exponential fusions are handled in one place.
  7. Current Operators calls the theory-level object an OperatorKind. This design keeps that name. The physics meaning is: \(b\), \(c\), \(X^\mu\), \(\partial X^\mu\), \(\psi^\mu\), \(e^{ikX}\), \(e^{q\phi}\), and a spin field \(S_A\) are operator kinds. A normal-ordered product such as \(:e^{-\phi}\psi^\mu:\) is not a new operator kind by default; it is an OperatorBody built from already defined kinds.
  8. Current Operators stores quantum numbers through tensor-code. This design keeps quantum numbers as a list, but separates their physics roles: additive charges, finite cyclic \(\mathbb{Z}_N\) charges, and representation labels may all occur in the list. Only representation-valued entries need tensor-code interpretation.
  9. Current Expressions has a coefficient times one local operator. This design differs by also requiring MultiOp: just a collection of local operators. Before an OPE or correlator is evaluated, an amplitude or SFT bracket naturally contains several local insertions at distinct points.
  10. Current Correlators names the tactics abstract, wick, and bosonization. This design keeps these tactics but makes two independent inputs explicit: the Riemann surface and the CFT/BCFT sector. A boundary condition is not a property of the surface; it defines a BCFT sector with its own boundary operator kinds. The same BCFT can be evaluated on different Riemann surfaces.
  11. Current OPE emphasizes deriving OPE coefficients from a basis and a pairing. This design keeps that as one strategy, but does not make it the only strategy. For each sector, the kernel chooses the highest-ranked strategy supported by all operators from that sector. Free-field RNS and ghost calculations can then use Wick directly, while abstract/interacting sectors can use basis-pairing or abstract tactics.
  12. Current Basis-Generation is a stub. This design differs by making basis generation a stream of states/operators with weights and quantum-number lists.
  13. Current SFT-Code says brackets use CFT OPEs and correlators. This design keeps that, but explicitly keeps BRST, PCO placement, local coordinates, propagators, and brackets outside the CFT kernel. They are physics layers that call the CFT kernel.

3.2. Extensions

  1. Extend Tensor-Code from one delta to a full tensor-structure language: sums of products of invariant tensors.
  2. Extend Tensor-Code with antisymmetrized gamma matrices and spinor bilinears. This is the tensor part of Ramond-sector spin-field OPEs.
  3. Extend Tensor-Code with an invariant-tensor generator and an independent-basis selector. This replaces the prototype’s overcomplete enumeration followed by probing with a typed kernel operation.
  4. Extend Operators with enough OperatorKind data to answer physics questions: statistics, conformal weights, quantum-number list, spacetime/internal index type, bulk/boundary domain, chiral support, and which OPE/correlator strategies the kind supports.
  5. Extend Operators with boundary insertions and boundary operator kinds. A boundary condition should give a BCFT sector: its boundary-local kinds, boundary-changing kinds when needed, and gluing rules from antiholomorphic bulk fields to holomorphic image fields.
  6. Extend OPE so that the public operation called ope is the projected OPE. There should not be separate public ope and projectedOpe operations. The projection data may ask for a target weight, target insertion point, and truncation order. This is needed for SFT brackets, BRST residues, PCO zero-mode actions, and spin-field OPEs.
  7. Extend Correlators with explicit surface-dependent zero-mode rules and Wick kernels. For example, the \(bc\) system on the sphere and free fields on a disk or upper half plane have base cases and propagators that are not determined by the operator kinds alone.
  8. Extend Basis-Generation with finite projections on generated spectra. GSO, orbifold, and orientifold projections should act linearly on the generated operator/state basis.

4. CFT Data Structures

4.1. Theory

A theory is a list of sectors and a list of operator kinds.

Examples of sectors:

  • \(bc\) ghosts;
  • the RNS chiral \((\psi^\mu,\phi,\eta,\xi)\) sector, equivalently \((\psi^\mu,\beta,\gamma)\) after bosonization;
  • free bosons \(X^\mu\);
  • heterotic gauge fermions \(\lambda^A\);
  • Narain lattice sectors;
  • an internal minimal model;
  • an abstract interacting CFT.

The tensor product of sectors is reflected in the quantum-number list carried by each operator kind and each generated operator. For example, an RNS vertex may carry entries for:

  • \(bc\) ghost number;
  • picture number;
  • fermion number or GSO charge as a finite cyclic \(\mathbb{Z}_N\) entry;
  • Lorentz representation;
  • internal CFT representation;
  • left/right sector labels.

Implementation sketch: 

pub const Sector = struct {
    name: []const u8,
    tactic_preference: []const StrategyId,
    statistics_rule: StatisticsRuleId,
    cocycle_rule: ?CocycleRuleId,
    pairing_data: ?PairingDataId,
};

pub const CFTSpec = struct {
    sectors: []const Sector,
    operator_kinds: []const OperatorKind,
    quantum_names: []const QuantumName,
    statistics_rules: []const StatisticsRule,
    cocycle_rules: []const CocycleRule,
    finite_projections: []const FiniteProjection,
    lattices: []const Lattice,
    local_rules: []const LocalRule,
    bcfts: []const BCFT,
};

4.2. Operator Kind

An OperatorKind is a physical kind of local field or primary.

Examples:

  • \(b\), \(c\);
  • \(\partial X^\mu\);
  • \(e^{ikX}\);
  • \(\psi^\mu\);
  • \(e^{q\phi}\), \(\eta\), \(\xi\);
  • \(S_A\), an R-sector spin field;
  • a minimal-model primary \(\phi_i\);
  • a pure-spinor variable \(\lambda^\alpha\), \(w_\alpha\), or current.

For each operator kind we need:

  • its name;
  • its sector;
  • whether it is a bulk or boundary operator;
  • for bulk operators, whether it is holomorphic, antiholomorphic, or has both holomorphic and antiholomorphic support;
  • for boundary operators, the support is chiral by construction;
  • the data needed by the sector’s statistics rule;
  • whether repeated identical factors vanish in a regular normal product;
  • its conformal weights, fixed or computed from labels such as momentum;
  • its list of quantum-number entries;
  • which spacetime/internal indices it carries;
  • which OPE/correlator strategies it supports.

The phrase “which spacetime/internal indices it carries” is meant literally: it says what kind of free indices can appear on this operator kind.

Implementation sketch: 

pub const OperatorKind = struct {
    name: []const u8,
    sector: SectorId,
    domain: OperatorDomain,
    support: ChiralSupport,
    statistics: StatisticsData,
    regular_square_zero: bool,
    weights: WeightData,
    quantum_numbers: []const QuantumEntry,
    indices: []const IndexSlot,
    strategies: []const StrategyId,
};

pub const OperatorDomain = enum {
    bulk,
    boundary,
};

pub const ChiralSupport = enum {
    holomorphic,
    antiholomorphic,
    holomorphic_antiholomorphic,
};

Here indices is physical data. For \(\partial X^\mu\) it says “one vector index”. For \(S_A\) it says “one spinor index”. For \(e^{ikX}\) it says “no free tensor index, but extra data stores the momentum”.

The two axes should not be collapsed. A bulk kind may be purely holomorphic like \(\partial X^\mu(z)\), purely antiholomorphic like \(\bar\partial X^\mu(\bar z)\), or mixed like \(:e^{ikX(z,\bar z)}:\). A boundary kind lives on the boundary coordinate and is chiral in the boundary CFT. Boundary conditions decide which boundary kinds exist; they are not encoded in OperatorDomain or ChiralSupport.

statistics is not just a fixed \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) flag. The sector interprets it. In the RNS \((\psi,\phi,\eta,\xi)\) sector, for example, \(\psi^\mu\), \(\eta\), and \(\xi\) are odd, while \(e^{q\phi}\) is odd for odd integral \(q\) and even for even integral \(q\). Half-integral charges need the sector’s cocycle rule to fix phases. This cannot be represented by a single boolean stored on the operator kind.

4.3. Strategy Selection

There is no separate class of operators that the OPE engine tries to peel off before choosing an algorithm.

Each operator kind says which OPE/correlator strategies it supports. Each sector gives a preference order for strategies. When the kernel is asked to evaluate operators from one sector, it chooses the first strategy in the sector’s preference list that is supported by every operator from that sector.

Examples of strategies:

  • Wick;
  • bosonize then Wick;
  • basis plus pairing;
  • abstract selection-rule filtering.

If an input contains operators from several sectors, the kernel groups them by sector, chooses the strategy independently in each sector, evaluates each sector factor, and then multiplies the sector results with the required signs and tensor/coordinate coefficients.

4.4. Sector Couplings

A sector is allowed to couple operator kinds that should not be split into independent CFT factors.

The main example is the RNS matter-fermion plus superghost system. Abstractly, the \(\beta\gamma\) system is tied by GSO and cocycles to the free fermions. The admissible physical R-sector ground states are not just \(\psi\) states or \(\phi\) states separately, but combinations such as \[ e^{-\phi/2}\Theta_A . \] Likewise the NS vertices include objects such as \[ :e^{-\phi}\psi^\mu: . \]

The sector must still allow subexpressions such as \(\psi^\mu\psi^\nu\) or \(e^{2\phi}\) to appear by themselves when a calculation asks for them. The point is only that strategy choice, statistics, cocycles, and GSO projection are sector-level data shared by the \(\psi,\phi,\eta,\xi\) operator kinds.

So the RNS chiral sector should contain at least:

  • \(\psi^\mu\);
  • \(\eta,\xi\);
  • \(e^{q\phi}\) or an abstract \(\phi\)-exponential kind;
  • matter spin fields \(\Theta_A,\Theta_{\dot A}\);
  • their normal-ordered products as OperatorBody values.

The same principle applies to heterotic gauge sectors. The \(32\) left-moving fermions \(\lambda^A\) form one chiral sector whose GSO projection produces the \(SO(32)\) theory. A different projection on two groups of \(16\) fermions produces the \(E_8\times E_8\) theory, with \(SO(16)\times SO(16)\) currents and spin fields enhancing the symmetry.

4.5. Statistics And Cocycles

Statistics are sector data evaluated on an OperatorBody. They are not just one boolean field attached to an atomic operator.

For the RNS chiral sector:

  • \(\psi^\mu\), \(\eta\), and \(\xi\) are odd;
  • \(e^{q\phi}\) is odd for odd integral \(q\), even for even integral \(q\);
  • half-integral \(\phi\)-charges and spin fields use the sector cocycle rule;
  • products such as \(:e^{-\phi}\psi^\mu:\) get their sign from the same sector rule, not from multiplying two independent CFT factors.

For bosonized fermions and Narain exponentials, the cocycle rule supplies the phase in the product of two exponentials. The kernel stores a small id for the generated sector procedure and compact labels for the lattice or bosonized charge vectors; the concrete formula is owned by the sector preset.

Implementation sketch: 

pub const StatisticsRule = struct {
    name: []const u8,
};

pub const CocycleRule = struct {
    name: []const u8,
};

pub fn bodyParity(spec: *const CFTSpec, body: OperatorBodyId) GrassmannParity;
pub fn cocyclePhase(
    spec: *const CFTSpec,
    left: OperatorBodyId,
    right: OperatorBodyId,
) Scalar;

This is deliberately small: statistics answers “does exchanging these bodies produce a minus sign?”, while the cocycle answers “what phase multiplies the bosonized or lattice exponential product?”. The public API does not expose these procedures; normalOrdered, ope, and correlator call them internally.

4.6. Lattice And Bosonized Labels

Some atomic bodies need labels in addition to their kind.

Examples:

  • \(e^{q\phi}\) carries the rational label \(q\);
  • \(e^{ikX}\) carries the momentum \(k\);
  • \(e^{i s_a H^a}\) for an \(SO(10)\) spin field carries a five-entry half-integral spin weight;
  • a Narain operator \({\cal O}_k\) carries a lattice vector \(k\in\Gamma\);
  • derivatives carry derivative labels.

These are labels of the atomic body, not new public API operations. In storage, the ExtraId on AtomicBody points to a compact list of labels.

Implementation sketch: 

pub const BodyLabel = union(enum) {
    none,
    rational_charge: Rational,
    momentum: MomentumId,
    bosonized_weight: ChargeVectorId,
    lattice_vector: LatticeVectorId,
    derivative: DerivativeLabel,
};

pub const Lattice = struct {
    name: []const u8,
    rank_left: u16,
    rank_right: u16,
    gram: MatrixId,
    basis_vectors: []const LatticeVectorId,
    cocycle: CocycleRuleId,
};

For a Narain sector, basis enumerates allowed lattice vectors and oscillator decorations subject to the query. The OPE of two lattice exponentials adds their lattice vectors and multiplies by the cocycle phase and coordinate factor. This is enough for \(\Gamma_{16}\), \(\Gamma_8\oplus\Gamma_8\), and ordinary toroidal Narain models.

4.7. Quantum Numbers

Quantum numbers are a list. This is how tensor products are represented in the CFT layer.

Each entry has a name and a value.

Examples: 

bc_ghost_number -> +1
picture -> -1
GSO -> 1 mod 2
orbifold_sector -> 3 mod 5
left_lorentz_rep -> chiral_spinor
right_lorentz_rep -> vector
minimal_model_primary -> (r,s)
pure_spinor_ghost_number -> +1

Implementation sketch: 

pub const QuantumEntry = struct {
    name: QuantumNameId,
    value: QuantumValue,
};

pub const QuantumValue = union(enum) {
    integer: i32,
    rational: Rational,
    cyclic: CyclicCharge,
    representation: tensor.RepresentationId,
    label: u32,
};

pub const CyclicCharge = struct {
    value: u32,
    modulus: u32,
};

The CFT kernel only adds, compares, and filters these entries. The physical meaning is supplied by the theory preset or by a client module.

4.8. Local Operator

A local operator is an insertion point together with a body.

The body is either:

  • the identity;
  • one atomic operator kind with labels;
  • a normal-ordered product of bodies.

Examples:

  • \(b(z)\);
  • \(\partial X^\mu(z)\);
  • \(e^{ikX}(z,\bar z)\);
  • \(:bc:(z)\);
  • \(:e^{-\phi}\psi^\mu:(z)\).

Implementation sketch: 

pub const Operator = struct {
    insertion: Insertion,
    body: OperatorBodyId,
};

pub const OperatorBody = union(enum) {
    identity,
    atomic: AtomicBody,
    normal_ordered: []const OperatorBodyId,
};

pub const AtomicBody = struct {
    kind: OperatorKindId,
    labels: ExtraId,
};

The body store must canonicalize normal products. It should:

  • flatten nested normal products;
  • move fields into canonical order;
  • compute Grassmann signs;
  • kill repeated regular fermions;
  • merge same-point bosonized exponentials when the theory says they fuse.

4.9. Multi-Local Operator

A multi-local operator is just a collection of local operators.

It has no extra physics beyond the local operators it contains. It is the natural input shape for an OPE or correlator before the algorithm has expanded several insertions around one target point or evaluated the vacuum expectation value.

Examples:

  • \(b(z)c(w)\);
  • \(V_1(z_1,\bar z_1)V_2(z_2,\bar z_2)V_3(z_3,\bar z_3)\);
  • the set of placed puncture operators appearing inside an SFT vertex before the local OPEs are taken.

Implementation sketch: 

pub const MultiOp = struct {
    operators: []const Operator,
};

The OPE and correlator APIs should accept this type. Passing a single local operator is the length-one case.

4.10. Domain And Chiral Support

The kernel should not use one field called locality for two different questions.

The two questions are:

  1. Does the operator live in the bulk or on a boundary?
  2. Which chiral components does the operator use?

Examples: 

bulk + holomorphic                  -> b(z), psi^mu(z), dX^mu(z)
bulk + antiholomorphic              -> bt(zbar), psit^mu(zbar), dXt^mu(zbar)
bulk + holomorphic_antiholomorphic  -> :exp(i k X(z,zbar)):
boundary + holomorphic              -> open-string or BCFT boundary field O(x)

Boundary operators are always chiral in the boundary CFT. Bulk operators may be chiral, antichiral, or mixed. BCFT gluing rules can rewrite an antiholomorphic bulk field as a holomorphic image field, but that is an operation performed in a BCFT correlator, not a change in the original operator kind.

4.11. Pairing And Local Data

The OPE engine is built from basis generation, correlators, and pairing data. For free sectors, the correlator strategy may itself use local Wick kernels. For abstract or interacting sectors, the correlator strategy may only apply selection rules or supplied conformal data.

Examples:

\[ b(z)c(w) \sim {1 \over z-w}, \]

\[ \partial X^\mu(z)\partial X^\nu(w) \sim -{\alpha' \over 2}{\eta^{\mu\nu}\over (z-w)^2}, \]

\[ \partial X^\mu(z)e^{ikX}(w,\bar w) \sim -{i\alpha'\over 2}{k^\mu\over z-w} e^{ikX}(w,\bar w). \]

Free-field correlator data may include short-distance kernels such as: 

b c -> 1/(z-w)
dX dX -> eta/(z-w)^2
dX expX -> k/(z-w) paired against the same expX body

Implementation sketch: 

pub const PairingData = struct {
    sector: SectorId,
    pairing: PairingId,
    inverse_pairing: InversePairingId,
};

4.12. Coefficient

A coefficient in a CFT calculation is:

  • a scalar;
  • a function of insertion-point differences;
  • a tensor structure.

Examples:

\[ {1\over z-w}, \qquad {\eta^{\mu\nu}\over (z-w)^2}, \qquad (z-w)^{k_1\cdot k_2}. \]

Implementation sketch: 

pub const Coeff = struct {
    terms: []const CoeffTerm,
};

pub const CoeffTerm = struct {
    scalar: Scalar,
    coordinate_factor: CoordinateFactor,
    tensor_factor: tensor.TensorExpr,
};

The coordinate factor must allow rational powers.

4.13. OPE

The public operation should be called ope, and it should be the projected OPE. There should not be a separate public OPE and OPEProjected.

Input:

  • any number of local operators, usually packaged as a MultiOp;
  • projection data, such as target weights and quantum numbers.

Output:

  • only those OPE terms that land at the requested weight.

The basic strategy for OPE is:

  1. use basis to generate candidate output OperatorBody values compatible with the requested weights and quantum numbers;
  2. compute the correlators needed to pair the input MultiOp against the dual basis elements;
  3. use the sector’s pairing data, or inverse Gram/BPZ matrix, to solve for the OPE coefficients;
  4. stream the resulting local terms.

If the input is multi-local, ope treats it as the product of all its local insertions. There is no need to expose a separate pairwise OPE operation. Derivative labels are just part of the operator data used by basis, correlator, and the pairing.

The strategy is chosen sector by sector: all operators in the same sector must share the strategy used for that sector, and the sector’s preference list decides which common strategy is tried first.

This is the operation needed by:

  • SFT brackets;
  • BRST residues;
  • PCO zero-mode actions;
  • Ramond spin-field OPE projection;
  • effective bracket computations.

4.14. Correlator

A correlator takes any number of local operators, usually packaged as a MultiOp.

It is evaluated with two independent pieces of data:

  • the Riemann surface or worldsheet geometry;
  • the CFT or BCFT sector whose operators are inserted.

The surface data says:

  • sphere, plane, torus, upper half plane, disk, or symbolic higher genus;
  • spin structure if needed;
  • puncture/local-coordinate information if the caller wants to carry it here;
  • which zero-mode structure and Wick kernels are used on that surface.

The BCFT data says:

  • which boundary condition or boundary sector defines the BCFT;
  • which boundary-local operator kinds exist;
  • which boundary-changing operator kinds exist, if any;
  • what gluing rules should be used for the doubling trick.

Implementation sketch: 

pub const Surface = struct {
    kind: SurfaceKind,
    spin_structure: ?SpinStructureId,
    wick_kernel_set: WickKernelSetId,
    zero_modes: ZeroModeRuleSetId,
};

pub const CorrelatorContext = struct {
    surface: Surface,
    sector: CorrelatorSector,
};

pub const CorrelatorSector = union(enum) {
    bulk_cft,
    bcft: BCFTId,
};

This keeps the Riemann surface separate from the CFT/BCFT sector. The surface mostly changes zero-mode structure and the precise form of Wick kernels. The BCFT sector supplies the boundary operator kinds and gluing data.

4.15. BCFT And Boundary Operators

A boundary condition gives a BCFT sector. That BCFT sector has its own boundary-local operator kinds.

For a single boundary condition \(B\), there is a boundary operator space \({\cal H}_{BB}\). For a pair of boundary conditions \(B_1,B_2\), there may be boundary-changing operators in \({\cal H}_{B_1B_2}\).

Implementation sketch: 

pub const BCFT = struct {
    name: []const u8,
    boundary_conditions: []const BoundaryCondition,
    boundary_operator_kinds: []const OperatorKindId,
    boundary_changing_kinds: []const BoundaryChangingKind,
    gluing_rules: []const GluingRule,
};

pub const BoundaryChangingKind = struct {
    from: BoundaryConditionId,
    to: BoundaryConditionId,
    kind: OperatorKindId,
};

The gluing rules tell the kernel how to rewrite antiholomorphic bulk fields as holomorphic image fields when the doubling trick applies.

For free bosons on a D-brane:

\[ \bar\partial X^\mu(\bar z) \mapsto R^\mu{}_\nu \partial X^\nu(\bar z). \]

For free fermions:

\[ \tilde\psi^\mu(\bar z) \mapsto \eta_B R^\mu{}_\nu \psi^\nu(\bar z). \]

The CFT kernel applies the gluing rule and then calls the ordinary chiral correlator/OPE engine.

5. Tensor-Code Data Structures

5.1. Representation And Index

tensor-code needs to know which representation an index belongs to.

Examples:

  • vector index \(\mu\) of \(SO(10)\);
  • chiral spinor index \(A\);
  • antichiral spinor index \(\dot A\);
  • adjoint index \(a\);
  • Chan-Paton fundamental index \(i\).

Implementation sketch: 

pub const Representation = struct {
    symmetry: SymmetryId,
    name: []const u8,
    label: RepresentationLabel,
};

pub const TensorIndex = struct {
    representation: RepresentationId,
    name: u32,
    variance: Variance,
};

5.2. Tensor Structure

A tensor structure is a sum of products of invariant tensors.

Examples:

\[ \eta^{\mu\nu}, \qquad (\Gamma^\mu C)_{AB}, \qquad \eta^{\mu\nu}\eta^{\rho\sigma} -\eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}, \qquad \epsilon_{\mu_1\ldots\mu_{10}}. \]

Implementation sketch: 

pub const TensorExpr = struct {
    terms: []const TensorTerm,
};

pub const TensorTerm = struct {
    scalar: Scalar,
    factors: []const TensorFactor,
};

pub const TensorFactor = union(enum) {
    metric: struct { left: TensorIndexId, right: TensorIndexId },
    delta: struct { left: TensorIndexId, right: TensorIndexId },
    epsilon: []const TensorIndexId,
    gamma_antisym: GammaAntisym,
    generator: GeneratorTensor,
    clebsch_gordan: ClebschGordanTensor,
};

5.3. Invariant Tensor Generation

The central problem for Type II R-sector amplitudes is:

Given external vector and spinor indices, generate all invariant tensors that can appear, then choose an independent basis.

Examples:

  • two spinor indices and one vector index give gamma-matrix structures;
  • many spinor/vector indices give products of gamma matrices and metrics;
  • amplitude calculations may require a very large invariant basis.

Implementation sketch: 

pub const InvariantProblem = struct {
    external_indices: []const TensorIndexId,
    required_symmetries: []const SymmetryId,
    symmetry_conditions: []const TensorSymmetryCondition,
};

The API should be: 

pub fn generateInvariants(problem: InvariantProblem, sink: Sink(TensorExpr)) !void;
pub fn selectIndependent(candidates: []const TensorExpr, sink: Sink(TensorExpr)) !void;
pub fn solveInBasis(basis: []const TensorExpr, target: TensorExpr) !LinearSolution;

The first serious target should be ten-dimensional vector/spinor structures for Type II flat space.

6. Stress Tests

These examples are the check that the data structures are broad enough without becoming a large public API.

6.1. RNS Chiral Sector

The holomorphic RNS matter-fermion plus superghost sector should be one sector, not a tensor product of an independent \(\psi\) sector and an independent \(\phi,\eta,\xi\) sector.

Atomic kinds:

  • \(\psi^\mu\), with one Lorentz-vector index;
  • \(\eta\) and \(\xi\);
  • \(e^{q\phi}\), with rational charge label \(q\);
  • \(\Theta_A\) and \(\Theta_{\dot A}\), with spinor index and optional bosonized \(H\)-weight labels.

Normal bodies:

  • \(:e^{-\phi}\psi^\mu:\);
  • \(e^{-\phi/2}\Theta_A\);
  • \(\psi^\mu\psi^\nu\);
  • \(e^{2\phi}\).

This directly matches the physics: physical RNS states are selected by a GSO projection on the combined \((\psi,\beta,\gamma)\) system, while intermediate operator bodies may still contain only the \(\psi\) part or only the \(\phi,\eta,\xi\) part.

The sector owns:

  • the Wick kernels for \(\psi^\mu\psi^\nu\), \(\eta\xi\), and \(\phi\phi\);
  • the statistics rule for \(\psi,\eta,\xi,e^{q\phi}\);
  • the cocycle rule for half-integral \(\phi\) charges and spin fields;
  • the finite GSO projection used by basis.

No extra public operation is needed.

6.2. SO(10) Spin Fields

The ten matter fermions can be bosonized by five \(H^a\)’s. A spin field has weights \(s_a=\pm 1/2\) and body label \(s=(s_0,\ldots,s_4)\), while tensor-code records whether its free index is chiral or antichiral.

The CFT part computes coordinate powers and cocycle phases. The tensor part expresses the answer in invariant tensors such as

\[ (\Gamma^{\mu_1\ldots\mu_p}C)_{AB}. \]

The important split is:

  • CFT labels know the bosonized \(H\)-charges used for contractions;
  • tensor indices know \(SO(10)\) vector, chiral-spinor, and antichiral-spinor representations;
  • basis generates candidate spin-field descendants by weight and quantum numbers;
  • ope solves their coefficients by correlator pairing.

This covers the prototype spin-field machinery without copying its old algorithm-specific organization.

6.3. Heterotic SO(32)

The \(32\) left-moving fermions \(\lambda^A\) are one chiral gauge sector.

Atomic kinds:

  • \(\lambda^A\), with an \(SO(32)\) vector index.

Normal bodies:

  • \(j^{AB}=:\lambda^A\lambda^B:\), represented as an OperatorBody with two antisymmetric vector indices, or as a named current kind if a preset wants a shorter basis.

The \(SO(32)\) GSO projection is a finite projection in BasisQuery. The sector’s Wick strategy contracts \(\lambda^A\lambda^B\) with \(\delta^{AB}\) and uses the sector statistics rule for signs. tensor-code supplies the vector and adjoint representation data.

6.4. Heterotic E8xE8

The same \(32\) real fermions can be split into two groups of \(16\) with separate fermion parities. The resulting CFT contains \(SO(16)\times SO(16)\) currents and weight-one spin fields that enhance the symmetry to \(E_8\times E_8\).

There are two equivalent presets:

  1. Free-fermion preset: lambda remains the atomic kind, and basis applies the two finite GSO projections. Spin fields are atomic kinds with \(SO(16)\) spinor indices and bosonized charge labels.
  2. Narain-lattice preset: the gauge sector is a rank-16 chiral lattice CFT with lattice \(\Gamma_8\oplus\Gamma_8\). The atomic exponential kind carries a lattice_vector label. Currents are root-vector exponentials and Cartan derivatives.

Both presets use the same public API. They differ only in sector data, operator kinds, labels, and cocycle rule.

6.5. Narain Cocycles

A Narain exponential is an atomic body with a lattice-vector label:

\[ {\cal O}_k = :e^{i k_L X_L+i k_R X_R}: . \]

The OPE adds labels:

\[ {\cal O}_k(z){\cal O}_{k'}(w) \sim \epsilon(k,k') (z-w)^{k_L\cdot k'_L} (\bar z-\bar w)^{k_R\cdot k'_R} {\cal O}_{k+k'}(w). \]

The kernel does not need to know why \(\Gamma_{16}\) and \(\Gamma_8\oplus\Gamma_8\) are the two rank-16 cases. It only needs the lattice, the bilinear form, and the cocycle rule. The theory preset enforces integrality, evenness, self-duality, and the finite projections appropriate to the string model.

7. Public CFT API

The public API should be small and physics-oriented. It should not grow one-off helpers such as a separate doubling operation. Boundary doubling is an implementation of ope or correlator when the selected sector is a BCFT. 

pub const OperatorBodyStore = struct {
    pub fn atomic(kind: OperatorKindId, labels: ExtraId) !OperatorBodyId;
    pub fn normalOrdered(factors: []const OperatorBodyId) !CanonicalOperatorBody;
};

pub const OpeProjection = struct {
    target_weights: ?ConformalWeights,
    quantum_constraints: []const QuantumConstraint,
};

pub fn ope(
    operators: MultiOp,
    projection: OpeProjection,
    sink: Sink(LocalTerm),
) !void;

pub fn correlator(
    context: CorrelatorContext,
    operators: MultiOp,
    sink: Sink(Coeff),
) !void;

pub fn basis(
    query: BasisQuery,
    sink: Sink(BasisState),
) !void;

Internally these functions should be methods on a generated theory type. The external meaning is still the physics list above.

BasisQuery should include weight conditions, quantum-number conditions, and optional finite projections. It should also allow optional domain and support filters, so a client can ask separately for bulk holomorphic, bulk antiholomorphic, bulk mixed, or boundary chiral candidates. That keeps projection as data, not another public verb.

8. How Future Modules Use It

8.1. On-Shell RNS

On-shell RNS code supplies:

  • vertex operators;
  • ghost and picture constraints;
  • GSO projection;
  • PCO insertion prescription;
  • integration over moduli.

It calls cft-code for OPEs and correlators. Here ope already means the projected OPE operation computed from candidate bodies, correlators, and pairing data. It calls tensor-code for Lorentz and spinor tensor algebra.

8.2. SFT

SFT code supplies:

  • local coordinate maps;
  • \(b\)-ghost mode insertions;
  • BRST operator;
  • BPZ pairing;
  • propagators;
  • bracket/vertex prescriptions.

It calls ope and correlator after placing operators with its local coordinate data. The placed operators are naturally passed as a MultiOp.

8.3. NLSM

NLSM code supplies:

  • target-space geometry;
  • background-field expansion;
  • regulator;
  • counterterms and beta functions.

It uses CFT operator kinds for fluctuation fields and background profiles, and uses tensor-code for target-index contractions such as curvature and \(H\)-flux tensors.

8.4. Pure Spinor

Pure-spinor code supplies:

  • constrained pure-spinor variables;
  • BRST operator;
  • quotient/gauge equivalence data;
  • regularization of the \(AdS_5\times S^5\) sigma model.

It uses cft-code for local OPE steps and tensor-code for representation constraints and invariant tensors.

9. First Milestone

The first milestone should be concrete:

  1. Define \(bc\), \(X^\mu\), and one combined RNS \((\psi^\mu,\phi,\eta,\xi)\) sector.
  2. Compute normal free-field OPEs with coefficients carrying tensor structures.
  3. Compute projected OPEs through the single public ope operation using basis generation, correlators, and pairing data.
  4. Generate a small R-sector spin-field tensor basis using gamma matrices.
  5. Solve one spin-field projected OPE in that tensor basis.
  6. Define a simple D-brane BCFT with boundary operator kinds and gluing rules, then derive one upper-half-plane two-point function using the surface-specific Wick kernel.
  7. Define a heterotic gauge-sector preset: first \(SO(32)\) with \(\lambda^A\), then either the free-fermion or Narain version of \(E_8\times E_8\).

If this works, the data structures are strong enough for the next layer of on-shell RNS and SFT work without baking those layers into the CFT kernel.

10. API Review

The proposed cft-code public surface is deliberately small:

  • OperatorBodyStore.atomic and OperatorBodyStore.normalOrdered construct OperatorBody values.
  • ope computes the projected OPE of any MultiOp using candidate bodies, correlators, and pairing data.
  • correlator evaluates any MultiOp in a surface and CFT/BCFT sector.
  • basis streams operator/state candidates satisfying a query.

This is concise because it avoids extra verbs for special cases:

  • no separate projectedOpe, because public ope is already projected;
  • no Taylor operation yet, because OPE is proposed through basis, correlator, and pairing data; derivative labels are already part of local operators;
  • no separate projectBasis, because finite projections belong in BasisQuery;
  • no doubleBulkInsertion or boundary-doubling helper, because gluing is how ope and correlator operate in a BCFT sector.

This is still complete enough because clients can build higher operations from these pieces:

  • BRST and PCO actions are residues/projections of OPEs with specified operator bodies.
  • SFT brackets are local-coordinate placement followed by ope and correlator.
  • On-shell amplitudes are correlators plus moduli integration supplied by the client.
  • Boundary and orientifold calculations are BCFT sectors, finite projections, and surface-dependent kernels.
  • R-sector computations use basis together with tensor-code invariant generation and solving.
  • Heterotic gauge sectors are either free-fermion sectors with GSO projections or Narain lattice sectors with cocycles; both use the same three public operations.

11. Summary

The corrected design is:

  • CFT tensor products are quantum-number lists.
  • Sectors may intentionally couple fields, as in the combined RNS \((\psi,\phi,\eta,\xi)\) sector.
  • A local operator is an insertion plus a body.
  • A normal product is a canonical OperatorBody, not a new OperatorKind.
  • Each OperatorKind declares supported strategies; each sector ranks strategies.
  • OPE/correlator evaluation chooses the best common strategy sector by sector.
  • Statistics and cocycles are sector-level rules evaluated on bodies and their labels.
  • A CFT coefficient is a coordinate factor times a tensor structure.
  • OPE is computed from candidate output bodies, correlators, and pairing data.
  • The public OPE operation is the projected OPE.
  • OPEs and correlators accept any number of local operators, usually through a MultiOp.
  • Surface data selects zero-mode structure and Wick kernels.
  • BCFT data supplies boundary operator kinds, boundary-changing kinds, and gluing rules.
  • Tensor-code is the engine for invariant spacetime/internal tensor structures.
  • Narain lattice data is internal sector data, not another public API layer.

This is the smallest kernel that still reaches on-shell RNS, SFT, NLSM, and pure-spinor applications.
