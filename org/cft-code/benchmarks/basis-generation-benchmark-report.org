#+title: Basis Generation Benchmark Report

Benchmark date: 2026-06-09.

Host: Linux =scheinpflug=, x86_64, kernel =7.0.10-arch1-1=.

Tools:

- Zig =0.16.0=, benchmark executable built with =-Doptimize=ReleaseFast=.
- Wolfram =14.3.0 for Linux x86 (64-bit) (July 8, 2025)=.
- Mathematica timeout cap: 15 seconds per case.

The benchmark harnesses are specified in
[[id:20260609T184500.000000][Basis Generation Deep Benchmark]].  The Zig side
times descriptor construction plus compact streaming.  The Mathematica side
uses held expressions; this matters because an unheld helper evaluates the
basis before =AbsoluteTiming= starts.

* Executive Summary

Mathematica crosses one second in the current Bosonic flat-space basis
workflow at modest weights:

- matter-only local operator output exceeds 1 second by weight 24 and times
  out at weight 32 under a 15-second cap;
- holomorphic Bosonic =generateBasisHolo[w,g]= exceeds 1 second by weight 12;
- level-matched closed Bosonic basis exceeds 1 second by total weight 8 and
  times out at total weight 12;
- non-level-matched closed Bosonic basis exceeds 1 second by total weight 6
  and is close to the timeout by total weight 8.

For same-count current-CFT compact enumeration, the Zig backend is already
orders of magnitude faster in ReleaseFast:

- free-boson matter counts are about 2,500x to 3,500x faster than Mathematica
  local-operator matter output for weights 12 through 28;
- holomorphic free-boson+\(bc\) counts matching =generateBasisHolo= are about
  1,100x to 2,600x faster for weights 8 through 16;
- closed-string compact product counts matching Mathematica closed basis rows
  are about 1,200x to 8,200x faster for the tested level-matched/all-splits
  cases;
- compact \(bc\) ghost enumeration is about 320x to 560x faster for the tested
  cumulative ranges, even though Mathematica remains under one second there.

The 10,000x target is not yet universal on this full-output audit benchmark.
The best closed all-splits rows are around 7,200x to 8,200x, and the
level-matched rows are around 1,200x to 2,500x.  The current bottleneck is no
longer level-matching recomputation; it is the remaining recursive traversal
and the benchmark sink's emitted-word hash.  The candidate still carries the
necessary compact output: presentation id, seed id/body, total weight, level,
per-component weights, quantum vector, and the full mode word.

The observed bottleneck is not the combinatorics alone.  The direct \(bc\)
prototype enumerator stays below one second through max weight 24, while
operator-producing Bosonic basis generation is already slow at much smaller
local-operator counts.  The dominant cost is symbolic local-operator
construction/canonicalization and retained expression lists.

* Methodology

The Zig benchmark emits rows:

#+begin_example
engine  name  category  weight  ghost  ns  count  max_depth  hash_or_bytes  scratch_bytes  status
#+end_example

=ns= is nanoseconds for descriptor construction plus streaming.  The sink only
counts candidates, tracks maximum word depth, and mixes a structural hash.  It
does not retain states.

Closed-string Zig rows use the same merged compact product presentation.  The
level-matched rows set =Query.level_match = .{}=, which requires component 0
and component 1 to carry equal total weights, including per-component seed
weights from \(bc\) zero-mode seeds.

The Mathematica benchmark emits rows:

#+begin_example
engine  name  category  weight  ghost  run  seconds  count  bytes  status
#+end_example

=bytes= is =ByteCount[result]= for completed cases.  Timeout rows report
=count=-1= and =bytes=-1=.  Cases are one cold sequential pass in one kernel,
followed by selected hot reruns.  The hot reruns intentionally test whether
prototype memoization removes the dominant cost.

This is a single-run engineering benchmark, not a statistical publication
benchmark.  The gaps are large enough that medians are not needed for the
conclusion; a future CI benchmark should still use repeated samples.

* Optimization Pass

The previous level-matching implementation recomputed component weights from
the emitted word whenever a candidate reached the sink.  That made every
level-matched candidate pay an extra scan over its mode word.  The optimized
backend now keeps per-component weights in the streaming context and mutates
them alongside the quantum vector on push/pop.  Seeds can provide
=component_weight_ticks=, so \(bc\) zero-mode seed weights are included in
closed-string level matching.

Two plausible optimizations were tested and rejected:

- a level-difference suffix table for level matching;
- rolling structural hashes stored for every prefix.

Both made the benchmark slower.  The suffix table added dense table build cost
without enough pruning at these weights.  Rolling hashes moved hash work from
emitted candidates to every prefix, which is worse because most prefixes do
not emit.  They were removed from both the Zig source and the org source.

The retained optimization is intentionally small: component weights are O(1)
state, and the level-match prefix predicate uses the remaining level budget as
a cheap admissibility bound.  This preserves the compact output and keeps the
data flow parallel-friendly: a worker owns one fixed scratch context, streams
borrowed candidate slices to the sink, and retains no basis list.

* Interpretation

1. The compact backend solves the right problem first.

   The prototype slows down when it lowers to symbolic local operators and
   retains lists.  The Zig backend keeps the hot path in compact fixed-size
   words and caller-owned scratch.  The large ratios appear even when counts
   are identical.

2. The main Mathematica bottleneck is symbolic output, not integer partitions.

   Hot reruns of selected public operator APIs remain over one second.  The
   cached substructures are not the dominant cost; rebuilding operator
   expressions and canonicalizing/retaining them is.  Byte counts in the
   multi-megabyte range appear at the same weights where runtime crosses one
   second.

3. The \(bc\) compact benchmark is the sanity check, not the stress case.

   The prototype compact \(bc\) generator is below one second through max
   weight 24, but the Zig backend is still hundreds of times faster and uses a
   fixed scratch context.  The serious slowdown appears when \(bc\) ghosts are
   joined with matter and lowered to local operators.

4. The previous Zig bottleneck was emitted-word rescanning.

   Level-matching used to recompute left/right component weights from the
   candidate word.  That was removed.  The stream context now maintains
   component weights incrementally, so level matching is a final O(1) equality
   check plus a cheap prefix admissibility bound.

5. Level matching belongs in the query, not in retained post-processing.

   The new =level_match= query filter checks emitted compact candidates during
   streaming.  It does not require materializing left and right sector bases or
   taking their Cartesian product.  Per-component seed weights are part of the
   seed data, so ghost zero-mode sectors are matched by total component weight,
   not merely oscillator level.

6. Operator lowering should remain outside enumeration.

   The data support the backend design in [[id:20260606T063253.759714][Basis-Generation]]:
   stream compact candidates first, then lower only the candidates required by
   the consumer.  For OPE projection, the next optimization should keep pairing
   on compact mode data as long as possible instead of eagerly creating full
   symbolic operator expressions.
